ABSTRACT

Phishing, online scams and digital payment fraud are among the most common cyber threats faced by ordinary citizens in India. People who use smartphones and UPI daily but have had little formal exposure to digital safety are especially vulnerable. This Community Engagement Program project, “Phishing, Scam & Fraud Awareness Survey”, was carried out with factory workers at Shreeram Chowk, Vitthalwadi(421306) to measure their current awareness of such threats, to raise that awareness through an interactive session, and to measure the change afterwards.

The project followed a three-stage method: a baseline (“before”) survey, a short awareness session covering phishing, lottery and job scams, bank/UPI fraud and safe practices, and an impact (“after”) survey. Fieldwork was completed over three days across four factories, and 34 workers responded. The same Google Form was used to collect data, which was analysed in a spreadsheet and presented as comparison charts.

The baseline survey showed low awareness: only 2 of 34 participants (5.9%) could correctly explain what phishing is, only 8 (23.5%) knew they should never share an OTP, UPI PIN or bank password with a caller, and 21 (61.8%) rated their own confidence in spotting a scam at 1 or 2 out of 5. After the session, all 34 participants (100%) defined phishing correctly, identified the warning signs of a fake message, refused to share OTP/PIN details, and chose to verify rather than click a suspicious link. Average self-rated confidence rose from 2.32 to 4.62 out of 5, and 32 participants (94.1%) rated the session as useful or very useful.

The results show that a short, practical, local-language-friendly session with real-life examples can quickly improve scam awareness among working communities. The report concludes with recommendations such as repeat sessions, printed multilingual safety cards, a community cyber-help point, and a follow-up survey to test long-term retention.

Keywords: Phishing, Online Scam, UPI Fraud, OTP Safety, Cyber Awareness, Community Engagement, Ulhasnagar

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION & OBJECTIVES

1.1 Introduction

India has seen a rapid rise in smartphone use and digital payments. UPI, mobile banking, WhatsApp and SMS are now part of everyday life, including for people who work in factories, shops and small businesses. This growth in digital convenience has also given cyber criminals a large number of new targets.

Phishing is a social-engineering attack in which a criminal pretends to be a trusted organisation such as a bank, a courier company or a government office, and tricks the victim into giving away sensitive information like passwords, OTPs, UPI PINs or card details. Scams such as fake lottery or prize messages, fake job offers that demand an upfront fee, fake customer-care numbers and fake bank or Aadhaar messages all rely on the same weakness: fear, greed or urgency makes people act before they think.

The Community Engagement Program (CEP) of the Department of Information Technology asks students to use their IT knowledge to solve real problems in local communities. For this project we selected the topic “Phishing, Scam & Fraud Detection Awareness Program” from the approved list and worked with factory workers in Shreeram Chowk, Vitthalwadi ..

1.2 Problem Statement / Community Gap

Many workers use mobile phones and UPI every day, but have rarely been taught how online fraud works. They often receive suspicious calls, SMS and WhatsApp messages, and many do not know how to recognise or respond to them. The technical gap is therefore not access to technology but the lack of practical awareness of how to use it safely. A single wrong click or a shared OTP can wipe out a worker’s savings, which is a serious loss for a family that depends on a daily or monthly wage.

1.3 Objectives

1.​ To measure the current awareness of phishing, scams and fraud among factory workers

through a baseline survey.

2.​ To teach participants to recognise phishing messages, fake lottery and job offers,

bank/UPI fraud and fake customer-care or Aadhaar calls.

3.​ To give simple, practical guidance on safe habits such as never sharing

OTP/PIN/passwords, verifying links and unknown calls, using strong PINs and reporting scams.

4.​ To measure the impact of the session with an after-session survey and compare it with the

baseline data.

5.​ To recommend practical next steps that can keep the community safe in the long term.

1.4 Scope of the Project

The project covered workers of four factories in Shreeram Chowk, Vitthalwadi(421306), over three field days. A total of 34 workers completed the surveys. The topics covered were phishing, lottery/prize scams, job-offer scams, bank/UPI scams, fake customer-care and Aadhaar-related calls, and safe digital practices. Findings relate to this group only and should not be generalised to all communities.

1.5 Significance

Prevention is far cheaper than recovery. Money lost to cyber fraud is often hard to recover, so making people aware before they are targeted is the most effective defence. The project also gave us hands-on experience of survey design, public communication, data analysis and reporting, which are important professional skills for IT graduates.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE / BACKGROUND

2.1 What is Phishing?

Phishing is a social-engineering attack where cyber criminals impersonate legitimate organisations to trick people into revealing sensitive information such as passwords, credit card numbers or personal data. Its common characteristics are messages that appear to come from trusted sources, fake websites that copy real ones, urgent or fearful language, and requests to click a link or download an attachment.

Table 1: Types of phishing attacks

Type	Description

Email phishing	Mass emails pretending to be from banks, payment services or technology companies, asking the user to verify account details.

Spear phishing	Targeted attacks on specific people, using personal information so that the message looks genuine.

Clone phishing	A copy of a genuine email, resent with malicious links or attachments.

Whaling	Phishing aimed at senior executives or important officials.

Vishing	Voice phishing through phone calls pretending to be from a bank or government office.

Smishing	Phishing through SMS text messages containing suspicious links.

2.2 Warning Signs of a Phishing Message

Awareness material used in this project identifies the following red flags:

•​ A sender address or website name that is slightly different from the real one (for example

“g00gle” instead of “google”).

•​ A generic greeting such as “Dear Customer” instead of the person’s name.

•​ Urgent or threatening language such as “Your account will be closed in 24 hours”.

•​ Links whose real address does not match the text shown.

•​ Requests for passwords, card numbers or OTPs. Banks never ask for these by email, SMS

or phone.

•​ Poor spelling or grammar, and unexpected attachments.

2.4 Financial Fraud and Digital Payments

Financial fraud includes credit-card fraud, UPI or mobile-payment fraud and online-banking fraud. In UPI fraud the criminal convinces the victim to approve a payment or reveal a PIN or OTP; no technical hacking is needed. The basic prevention rules are simple: never share a UPI PIN or OTP, enable transaction alerts, use biometric lock where available, avoid doing transactions on public Wi-Fi, and check bank statements regularly. For online banking, strong and unique passwords and two-factor authentication add a second layer of protection.

2.5 Protecting Personal Information and Passwords

Participants were taught what should never be shared online: passwords, PINs and security answers, bank or card numbers, OTPs, and identity numbers such as Aadhaar and PAN. A strong password is at least 12 characters long, mixes upper and lower case letters, numbers and symbols, avoids personal details and is unique for each account. Two-factor authentication (OTP, authenticator app or biometric) means that a stolen password alone is not enough to take over an account.

2.6 Reporting and Help Available in India

Victims of cyber fraud are advised to act immediately: change affected passwords, call the bank using its official number to block cards or accounts, monitor statements, and file a complaint. In

India, complaints can be filed on the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal (cybercrime.gov.in) or by calling the cyber-crime helpline 1930, and at the local police cyber-crime cell. Reporting early improves the chance that money can be frozen.

2.7 Research Gap

General awareness material is widely available, but it is often in English, text-heavy and aimed at urban office users. Factory workers have limited time and few chances to attend training. This project therefore used a short, discussion-based session with everyday examples (OTP, lottery SMS, job fee, customer-care calls) and measured its effect through a before-and-after survey.

2.8 Source Material

The session content was based on the departmental teaching material “Phishing, Scam & Fraud Detection Awareness Program – Teaching Material for CEP”, which has six modules: understanding phishing, understanding online scams, understanding financial fraud, practical security measures, what to do if you are a victim, and interactive case studies.

CHAPTER 3: METHODOLOGY & COMMUNITY

INTERACTION / FIELD WORK

3.1 Research Design

The project used a pre-test / post-test design. The same group of participants answered a baseline survey, attended an awareness session and then answered an impact survey. Comparing the two sets of answers shows what changed after the intervention. Both surveys were built in a single Google Form with two sections, “Before the session” (15 questions) and “After the session” (14 questions).

3.2 Target Community

The target group was factory workers at Shreeram Chowk, Vitthalwadi(421306). Workers in this area use smartphones, WhatsApp and UPI regularly. A total of 34 workers took part. Participation was voluntary and no personal financial information was collected.

3.3 Survey 1 (Baseline) Design

The baseline section asked participants about their internet use, their knowledge of the word “phishing”, their ability to recognise warning signs, how they would react to a lottery message, a job offer that asks for money and a suspicious link, their phone-security habits (screen lock and password strength), whether they would share OTP/PIN details, and how confident they felt about spotting an online scam (scale 1–5). Questions were multiple-choice and written in simple English so that they were easy to answer. The full question list is in Appendix B.

3.4 Field Execution (Awareness Session)

After the baseline survey, we conducted a short interactive session in each factory. The session followed the teaching material and covered:

•​ What phishing is and the warning signs of a fake message (urgency, unknown sender,

requests for OTP/password/bank details).

•​ Lottery/prize scams, job-offer scams, bank/UPI scams, fake customer-care calls and fake

government/Aadhaar messages.

•​ Real-life style case studies such as a fake bank verification message, a free-prize message

that asks for a “delivery fee”, and a fake tech-support pop-up.

•​ Safe practices: never share OTP/UPI PIN/password, verify unknown calls and links, use

strong PINs and screen lock, never pay money for a job or lottery offer, and report or block suspicious messages.

•​ What to do if you are a victim: call the bank on its official number, change passwords and

report at cybercrime.gov.in or helpline 1930.

3.5 Survey 2 (Impact) Design

Immediately after the session, participants answered the “After the session” section. It repeated the key knowledge questions (meaning of phishing, warning signs, sharing OTP, handling a suspicious message), added scenario questions (a ₹50,000 prize SMS that asks for ₹2,000, a job offer that asks for ₹5,000, a call asking for Aadhaar/OTP), and asked about confidence, preparedness, planned safe practices, usefulness of the session and the likelihood of using what was learned.

3.7 Data Collection and Tools

Responses were collected through Google Forms and exported to a spreadsheet. Answers were coded as correct or incorrect for each knowledge question, and counts and percentages were calculated for the before and after sections. Charts were prepared from these counts. Because the

“before” and “after” sections were filled by the same participant in one form, each participant’s two sets of answers could be compared directly.

3.8 Ethical Considerations

•​ Participation was voluntary and participants were told the purpose of the project.

•​ No OTP, PIN, password, account number or other sensitive financial data was collected. •​ Genuine responses only were used; no data was created or changed.

•​ Photographs and videos were taken only with the consent of participants and the factory

management.

•​ We maintained professional conduct as representatives of our college.

3.9 Limitations

•​ The sample is small (34 workers) and from one area, so results cannot be generalised. •​ The after-session survey was taken right after the session. It shows immediate learning,

not long-term retention.

•​ Answers are self-reported, and a few participants may have been influenced by the

session or by the group around them.

•​ Several questions were multiple-choice, so participants could have guessed the correct

option.

CHAPTER 4: DATA ANALYSIS & VISUALIZATIONS

This chapter presents the results of the baseline and impact surveys. All percentages are calculated out of 34 respondents unless stated otherwise.

4.1 Participant Profile

Figure 1: Age group of participants

Half of the participants (17, or 50.0%) were aged 26–40 and about a third (11, or 32.4%) were aged 18–25. All participants use the internet: 26 (76.5%) every day and 8 (23.5%) a few times a week. Heavy daily use of phones and the internet makes this group a realistic target for scam messages.

4.2 Baseline Survey: Awareness Before the Session

Knowledge of phishing

Figure 2: Have you heard the word “phishing”? (before)

Only 7 participants (20.6%) said they had heard the word “phishing”; 18 (52.9%) had not and 9 (26.5%) were not sure.

Figure 3: What does phishing mean? (before)

Only 2 participants (5.9%) chose the correct meaning. 18 (52.9%) thought phishing is a way to improve internet speed, 8 (23.5%) thought it means sending photos online and 6 (17.6%) were not sure.

Recognising warning signs and scams

Only 6 participants (17.6%) knew that all of the listed items (a request for OTP/password, an “act now” threat and an unknown sender) are warning signs. Individually, 14 (41.2%) picked only the OTP/password request, 8 (23.5%) picked only the urgency message and 6 (17.6%) picked only the unknown sender. When asked which items can be a scam, only 5 (14.7%) said that fake lottery messages, fake job offers and fake bank/UPI messages can all be scams. 20 participants (58.8%) said they had already received a suspicious message, call or email, and 12 (35.3%) were not sure whether they had.

Behaviour in risky situations

Figure 4: Response to a lottery message asking for money (before)

Faced with a lottery message that asks for money first, only 15 participants (44.1%) would ignore it and check whether it is genuine. 10 (29.4%) would pay the money, 6 (17.6%) would share their bank details and 3 (8.8%) would ask the sender for their OTP. For a job offer that asks for money first, only 11 (32.4%) would check the company and the job first; 11 (32.4%) would pay immediately, 6 (17.6%) would share their OTP and 6 (17.6%) would give their bank password.

Figure 5: Response to a suspicious link on WhatsApp or SMS (before)

Only 8 participants (23.5%) would not click a suspicious link and would check who sent it. 11 (32.4%) would click it to check, 9 (26.5%) would forward it to others and 6 (17.6%) would enter their details to see what it is. Forwarding and clicking both help scams spread. Regarding OTP safety, only 8 participants (23.5%) correctly said that they should not share an OTP, UPI PIN or bank password with a caller; 16 (47.1%) said they should and 10 (29.4%) were not sure.

Phone security habits

Figure 6: Screen lock and password habits (before)

19 participants (55.9%) use a screen lock, PIN or password on their phone, while 11 (32.4%) do not and 4 (11.8%) were not sure. Only 10 (29.4%) use different or strong passwords or PINs for important accounts, 13 (38.2%) do so sometimes and 11 (32.4%) do not.

Confidence before the session

Average self-rated confidence in identifying an online scam was only 2.32 out of 5. 21 participants (61.8%) rated themselves 1 or 2, 10 (29.4%) rated 3 and only 3 (8.8%) rated 4 or 5.

4.5 Confidence and Preparedness

Figure 8: Self-rated confidence before and after the session

Average confidence in identifying an online scam rose from 2.32 to 4.62 out of 5, an increase of 2.30 points. After the session 23 participants (67.6%) rated themselves 5 and 9 (26.5%) rated 4, so 32 (94.1%) were highly confident. Confidence increased for 32 participants and stayed the same for 2; it did not drop for anyone. When asked how prepared they felt to deal with a scam, 29 participants (85.3%) rated themselves 4 or 5 (average 4.38 out of 5).

4.6 Reaction to the Session

Figure 9: Usefulness of the session and likelihood of using what was learned

19 participants (55.9%) found the session “very useful”, 13 (38.2%) found it “useful” and 2 (5.9%) “somewhat useful”; nobody rated it “not useful”. All 34 participants (100%) rated the likelihood of using what they learned in daily life as 4 or 5 (average 4.85 out of 5). Asked whether they had changed anything they do online after the session, 26 (76.5%) said yes, 5 (14.7%) were not sure and 3 (8.8%) said no.

4.7 Key Findings

•​ Baseline awareness was very low: only about 6% understood phishing and about a

quarter would refuse to share an OTP or avoid a suspicious link.

•​ Risky habits were common: nearly half of the participants would share an OTP/PIN with

a caller, and many would pay money for a lottery or job offer.

•​ A short interactive session raised correct responses to 100% on nearly every question,

and confidence more than doubled.

•​ The “forward it to friends” habit survived in 3 participants, showing that spreading

suspicious messages needs special emphasis.

•​ Participants valued the session and said they would use it: 94.1% found it useful or very

useful and everyone rated future use as 4 or 5.

4.8 IT Solutions Proposed

•​ A one-page multilingual (Hindi/Marathi/English) “Scam Safety Card” with simple icons

for OTP, links, lottery and job-fee scams.

•​ A WhatsApp broadcast or group in the factory for short weekly scam alerts and

reminders.

•​ Quick-reference posters at notice boards showing helpline 1930 and the portal

cybercrime.gov.in.

Note on interpretation: the after-session results were collected immediately after teaching, so they reflect what participants could recall and choose straight after the session. A follow-up after a few weeks would be needed to confirm lasting behaviour change.

CHAPTER 5: CONCLUSIONS & RECOMMENDATIONS

5.1 Conclusions

This project set out to measure and improve awareness of phishing, scams and fraud among factory workers in Shreeram Chowk, Vitthalwadi .. The baseline survey showed that although the workers use the internet and phones daily, most did not know the meaning of phishing, could not identify all warning signs and often chose unsafe actions such as paying money, sharing OTPs or clicking suspicious links.

After a short interactive awareness session, every participant gave the correct answer on the core questions, average confidence rose from 2.32 to 4.62 out of 5, and 94.1% rated the session as useful or very useful. These results indicate that simple, practical and example-based teaching is an effective way to improve cyber-safety awareness among working communities.

Each of the five objectives was achieved: current awareness was measured, scam types and warning signs were taught, safe practices were explained, the impact was measured through the second survey, and recommendations were prepared. The project also showed that awareness is a first step: habits such as forwarding messages or reusing weak PINs need continued reinforcement.

5.2 Recommendations

For the community / factories

•​ Hold a short refresher session every few months, with new scam examples, so that

awareness does not fade.

•​ Display a Scam Safety Card and helpline 1930 / cybercrime.gov.in on notice boards in

each factory.

•​ Appoint a “cyber-safety buddy” in each factory who can answer simple questions and

help colleagues check suspicious messages.

•​ Encourage workers to turn on screen lock, UPI app lock and transaction alerts, and to use

different, strong PINs for important accounts.

For future CEP students

•​ Run a follow-up survey 3–4 weeks after the session to test long-term retention.

•​ Include a hands-on practice segment (for example, checking a sample link or UPI collect

request on a demo screen).

•​ Prepare the teaching material and survey in Hindi and Marathi for even better

understanding.

•​ Collect a “first-name / roll-number” code instead of full names to protect privacy while

keeping before/after matching possible.

Technical recommendations

•​ Voice-based or video micro-lessons in local languages that can be shared on WhatsApp.

•​ A simple community cyber-help point (a helpline number or a messaging group) where

people can send a suspicious message for a quick check.

•​ Use of call-blocking and spam-filter features and of the official Sanchar Saathi /

cyber-crime reporting channels to report suspicious calls and messages.

5.3 Future Scope

The programme can be extended to other groups such as senior citizens, shopkeepers, women self-help groups and students. A larger sample and a longer follow-up period would allow a more reliable statistical assessment of how well awareness translates into safer behaviour.
